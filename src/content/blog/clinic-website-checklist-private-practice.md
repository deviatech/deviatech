A private clinic website should explain what the practice offers, introduce its practitioners, show where and how appointments take place, and give visitors a clear way to get in touch. For most solo practices, one well-managed enquiry route is enough to launch. Clinics with several practitioners or locations also need to show who provides each service, where it is available and who handles enquiries.

Use this checklist to brief a designer or assess your current site. It is written for private practice owners in Pakistan and Europe, with examples for psychology and therapy practices.

## 1. Make the homepage answer the basics

Name the practice, what it offers, the people it serves and the city or area it covers. Say whether appointments are in person, online or both. Make the next step visible on a phone without asking visitors to read a long introduction first.

A fictional example: “Child psychology appointments in Lahore. See our team, find the clinic and request an appointment.” Use that level of specificity with facts your practice can verify.

## 2. Give each distinct service enough room

A useful service page answers three questions: who the service is for, what someone can expect at their first visit and how to enquire. Add separate pages when that information genuinely differs. Avoid publishing near-identical pages for every city or neighbourhood you want to appear in.

## 3. Show people who they might see

Give each practitioner a consistent profile: role, verified qualifications, services, location, appointment languages and whether they see people online, in person or both. If the clinic matches people with a practitioner, say how that works. For a solo practice, a detailed About page can cover this.

Our [Little Fieldnotes concept](https://deviatech.com/templates/little-fieldnotes) is a design example for a fictional child psychology practice. Use it to discuss tone and layout, not as evidence of client outcomes.

## 4. Make location, language and fees easy to find

Put essential details near the action: address or online service area, opening hours, contact options and first-visit instructions. If you publish fees, show the currency and what the fee covers. For multiple locations, show which practitioners and services are available at each one.

For a practice in Pakistan, give the city and neighbourhood, plus an address or useful landmark. Offer a call or WhatsApp option only if someone is responsible for monitoring it. If you serve more than one European market, make the website language, appointment languages and actual areas served explicit. An English site does not mean every visitor needs or can book an English-language appointment.

## 5. Choose a contact process your team can run

| What you can support | Button label | What happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff answer questions or return calls | Contact the practice | The team replies using the stated channel. |
| Staff check availability manually | Request an appointment | The visitor gets an acknowledgement. Staff confirm a time later. |
| The website confirms slots from live availability | Book an appointment | The system confirms the selected time and provides the next steps. |

If staff manage requests manually, decide who receives them and who covers them when that person is away. Test that a request actually reaches the team. A successful message on the screen is not proof of delivery.

## 6. Keep the first contact short

Ask for the minimum needed to respond. If a callback is the goal, a name and a contact method may be enough. Keep detailed clinical intake separate from a general website enquiry and never put enquiry text or contact details into analytics or tracking links.

Review who receives requests, where they are stored and when they are deleted. Where the GDPR applies, the [European Commission’s data-minimisation guidance](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/principles-gdpr_en) is a starting point. The applicable requirements depend on the practice and how it handles data.

## 7. Test two first-visit journeys

Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to complete both tasks on a phone:

- **Find the right person.** Start on a service page and identify a practitioner, their location and the languages they offer.
- **Make contact.** Start on the home page, send a test request and check that the on-screen message matches what the team actually receives.

Also test with a keyboard. Check visible focus, clear field labels and errors that say what to fix. The [W3C’s accessibility design guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tips/designing/) is a useful reference.

## Common questions

### How many pages does a private practice website need?

There is no fixed number. A solo practice can often start with Home, About, Services and Contact, with fees and visit details where they are most useful. Separate profiles and location pages become worthwhile when people have real choices to compare.

### Do I need online booking before launch?

No. A monitored number or a well-run request form can be enough. Consider live booking when staff can keep slots current, handle changes and make the confirmation process clear.

### What should I give a web designer before asking for a quote?

Send your existing website if you have one, a list of services and practitioners, locations and appointment languages, how you handle first contact today, and what you want to improve. Do not send patient information. Ask each designer to list what their quote includes: who owns the domain, what staff can update themselves, what support is included and which tools cost extra. List content writing, translation, redirects from an old website and calendar integration separately so you can compare like with like.
