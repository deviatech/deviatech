Every outsourcing conversation eventually gets to cost, but cost isn't actually the question that decides whether it works out. Plenty of cheap outsourcing arrangements fail. The ones that hold up share a few things in common: the client can see progress, ownership is never in question, and communication doesn't depend on a single overworked project manager relaying messages in both directions.

That's the version of outsourcing we try to run from Lahore.

## What the concerns usually are

Founders and companies looking at Pakistan as a delivery base tend to ask some version of the same three questions.

**Will communication actually work?** Pakistan sits at UTC+5, which gives a real working overlap with UK mornings and a smaller one with the US East Coast. We agree on working hours and update cadence per client rather than defaulting to whatever's convenient for us.

**Will I actually own what gets built?** This is usually the concern under the concern. [We build every project the way we'd want to buy one](/): a fixed price agreed before code is written, and full ownership, the repository, the deployment, everything, once final payment clears. No holding code hostage, no forced hosting lock-in.

**Will quality hold up without me watching every day?** Weekly working increments instead of a single reveal at the end. If something's off, you see it in week two, not week eight.

## Where this fits and where it doesn't

A dedicated team arrangement works well when there's already a product owner or technical lead on the client's side who can make prioritization calls. It works less well when a client wants a fully managed department from day one, with no involvement of their own, that's a different kind of engagement than what a small agency can responsibly promise.

The same logic applies to project-based outsourcing. Fixed-price work suits a scope that can be written down before it starts. Open-ended product work with a moving roadmap usually needs an hourly or capacity arrangement instead, as long as there's still weekly visibility into what those hours produced.

## Starting small

Most engagements, whether project-based or a dedicated team extension, start with a small onboarding scope. That gives both sides a real basis for deciding whether to scale up, instead of committing to months of work based on a sales call alone.
