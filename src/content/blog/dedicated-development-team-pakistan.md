Most "dedicated team" pitches are really a rate card with names attached. That tells you almost nothing about whether the arrangement will work.

What decides the outcome is duller: whose backlog it is, who reviews the code, how often you see working software, and what you're left holding if the engagement ends. Pakistan has real technical capacity. The structure around it is what you're actually buying.

## When this model fits, and when it doesn't

It works when you already have a product owner, a prioritised backlog, a repository, and enough runway to need capacity for several delivery cycles.

It doesn't work when the product problem is still unsettled. Adding developers to an unclear scope produces expensive churn. If that's where you are, a discovery or fixed-scope project comes first, and [MVP development](/mvp-development) is the better starting point.

## What to actually check

**Technical fit, specifically.** A list of technologies on a website proves nothing. Ask what the team personally shipped in your stack. We work in React, Next.js, Go, Electron, Laravel and Docker, and the [Cvilo case study](/case-studies/cvilo-ai-resume-builder) is a Next.js and React product with a Go backend and AI features, which is a more useful answer than a logo grid.

**Communication, in detail.** Who attends planning? Where do questions get recorded so they don't live in one person's inbox? Which hours of overlap are guaranteed, not hoped for? A weekly update is only worth having if it shows working software and the decisions behind it.

**Ownership, in writing.** You keep access to the repository, deployment, analytics and project context. Agree now who opens pull requests, who reviews them, what gets documented, and what handover looks like if either side walks away. This conversation is easy at the start and miserable later.

**Capacity, not rate.** Price follows seniority, weekly capacity, engagement length, technical ownership, and whether the team is expected to join product decisions. Compare the usable delivery capacity you get, minus the review work you'll have to do yourself. Two cheap developers who need constant correction are not cheaper.

## Start small on purpose

Review the codebase and backlog, agree the team shape and working hours, then run one small delivery cycle before committing to anything larger. A first cycle tells you more about a partner than any interview, and it's a cheap way to find out you were wrong.

Only scale up after the team understands both your product and your review process.
